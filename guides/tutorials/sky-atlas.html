Find your way around / Step 01

Open Sky Atlas

Start here Start CAst. If you are in another workspace, use Mode → Choose Mode… to return to the launcher.

  1. Under Explore, Visualize & Process, click the Sky Atlas card (1).
What you should see

The sky replaces the launcher. Stars are drawn as points, while circles mark cataloged objects. You can explore immediately, without opening an image folder.

Find the hidden controls →Find an object →Show my own image → Browse all steps

Find your way around / Step 02

Reveal the bottom controls

Start here Open Sky Atlas.

  1. Move the pointer to the bottom center of the sky and leave it there for a moment. A row of square buttons slides up in the outlined area (1).
  2. Hold the pointer over an icon to read its tooltip. Move back into the sky to let the controls slide away.
What you should see

The first button is a magnifying glass. The bar also contains the clock, observing site, sky layers and settings. If it disappears, move the pointer back to the bottom center of the atlas.

Move around the sky →Look up a target →Set my observing site →Choose a coordinate grid →Control the catalogs →Use an image overlay → Browse all steps

Find your way around / Step 03

Pan across the sky and zoom

Start here Open the atlas. Use this whenever you want to explore by eye.

  1. Place the pointer in the marked part of the sky (1). Hold the left mouse button, drag a short distance and release.
  2. Roll the mouse wheel forward to zoom in, or backward to see a wider area. Look at FOV along the bottom edge of the map. FOV means field of view.
What you should see

Dragging brings different stars into the frame. A smaller FOV spans fewer degrees of sky and lets you distinguish objects that previously appeared close together.

Center on a named object →Recover the opening view →Identify constellations → Browse all steps

Find a target and read its details / Step 04

Open the sky search

Start here Reveal the bottom controls.

  1. Click the magnifying glass at the left end of the bar (1). Its tooltip says Search the sky.
What you should see

A search window opens with a box for a name, catalog ID or alias. It can find entries in downloaded catalogs even when their map layers are hidden.

Find the Orion Nebula →Find the Moon → Browse all steps

Find a target and read its details / Step 05

Find the Orion Nebula (M42)

Start here The search window is open.

  1. Click the search box (1) and type M42. Wait for the results to appear.
  2. Click the Nebula Orion Nebula row (2), or press Enter while that row is highlighted. Check that its catalog line says Messier.
What you should see

The search closes, the chart centers on the Orion Nebula and an information panel appears at upper left. The number of other matches depends on which catalogs are installed.

Read the coordinates →Check visibility from my site →Identify the coordinate lines → Browse all steps

Find a target and read its details / Step 06

Read where the object is

Start here Find M42, or locate a labeled star or object in the atlas.

  1. Click the object’s marker in the sky (1) to pin it. Double-click a visible marker if you also want to center it.
  2. Read the panel at upper left (2). RA and Dec locate the object on the celestial sphere. Alt tells you how far above or below the local horizon it is. Az gives its compass direction.
What you should see

At Greenwich on 6 January 2026 at 23:00 UTC, this example places M42 about 33° above the horizon toward the south. Your Alt and Az depend on your own site and time.

Open an object reference →Change the observing site →Try another hour →Inspect the Moon → Browse all steps

Find a target and read its details / Step 07

Open a catalog entry or research papers

Start here Pin a named object.

  1. In the marked row below the object type (1), click SIMBAD for an object record, ADS for papers, or VizieR for catalog data.
  2. Return to CAst when you want to continue exploring the map.
What you should see

Your browser opens the chosen service with the object identifier. The services need an internet connection. The links available depend on the selected object.

Find a target and read its details / Step 08

Search for the Moon

Start here Open sky search. Set the site and UTC time first if you want a view for a particular night.

  1. Type Moon in the search box (1).
  2. Select Natural Satellite Moon under Solar System (2).
What you should see

The atlas centers on the Moon’s position for the selected site and time and pins its details. At a wide field of view it may appear only as a small disk.

Zoom in on the lunar disk →Change the observing date → Browse all steps

Find a target and read its details / Step 09

Inspect the lunar disk

Start here Select the Moon. If the Moon is below your horizon, turn off the ground layer to inspect it anyway.

  1. Place the pointer over the lunar disk (1) and roll the wheel forward a few notches at a time. If the disk slips toward an edge, drag to bring it back toward the middle.
  2. Roll backward when you want to see the surrounding field again.
What you should see

The lit part of the Moon fills more of the view. Its shape and orientation follow the selected observing time and site.

Look on a different date →Return to the wide sky → Browse all steps

Plan for a place and time / Step 11

Set your observing site

Start here Open Observer Location. Use your own coordinates or the Greenwich example shown.

  1. In Name (1), enter a label. In Latitude (2) and Longitude (3), enter degrees. South and west use negative values. For this example enter Greenwich, 51.48, and 0.00.
  2. Enter Elevation in metres (4), for example 46. Leave Keep this site in quick-switch presets checked if you want to use it again. For a previously saved site, pick it under Saved Sites and click Load instead.
  3. Click Save Location (5).
What you should see

The window closes and the atlas footer shows the active site. Altitude, azimuth and the horizon now refer to that location.

Set the observing time →See the local horizon →Read the target’s altitude → Browse all steps

Plan for a place and time / Step 12

Freeze the sky and open the clock

Start here Reveal the dock.

  1. Look at the third button from the left (1). If its tooltip says Pause time, click it. When the clock is paused, the button shows a play triangle, as in the screenshot.
  2. Click the Date and time button immediately to its left (2).
What you should see

A UTC date and time editor appears above the dock. Pausing makes it easier to compare two moments while holding the sky still.

Enter a time or advance one hour →Inspect the frozen chart → Browse all steps

Plan for a place and time / Step 13

Choose a UTC date or step one hour

Start here Open the date and time popup.

  1. Click the date and time field (1). Select and edit each part, or open the calendar to pick the date. For the Orion example, set 6 January 2026, 23:00 UTC.
  2. Click +1 h (2) to jump one hour forward. Use -1 h to undo that jump. +1 d and -1 d change the day.
  3. For the Orion example, click outside the popup to keep the selected time. If you want to return to the present, click Now (3) before closing the popup. Click the play triangle on the dock when you want time to advance again.
What you should see

The UTC field changes to the selected moment. With a target pinned, compare Alt and Az before and after the hour jump.

Read the altitude at this time →Find the Moon at this time →Look at the horizon → Browse all steps

Choose map layers and catalogs / Step 14

Choose the kind of grid

Start here Reveal the dock.

  1. Click Toggle equatorial grid (1) for right ascension and declination lines. Click it again to hide them.
  2. Click Toggle alt-az grid (2) for the altitude and azimuth lines at your observing site. For an easy comparison, show one grid at a time.
What you should see

Each button switches its own grid. Use equatorial coordinates to match a catalog position and local coordinates to decide where to look from the ground.

Compare both grids on Orion →Show the ground →Show constellation lines → Browse all steps

Choose map layers and catalogs / Step 15

Compare the two grids on one field

Start here Find M42 and zoom out enough to see several stars. For the same sky orientation, use the example site and time.

  1. In the atlas, use the marked grid button (1) for the view you want. If the other grid is still visible, click its button to turn it off.
  2. Use Equatorial and Local horizon above the tutorial screenshot to compare the same frozen sky. Follow a bright star as you switch diagrams.
What you should see

The stars keep their positions in this comparison. The two grids run in different directions because one follows Earth’s rotation axis and the other follows the observer’s horizon.

Show the ground →Trace constellations → Browse all steps

In the Orion field, the lines help you trace the pattern around the nebula even when other catalogs are crowded.

Choose map layers and catalogs / Step 17

See which objects are above the ground

Start here Set your site and UTC time. Reveal the bottom dock.

  1. Click Toggle ground and horizon (1). Drag the map or zoom out until the horizon crosses the frame.
  2. Look above the horizon line for visible sky. If a selected object falls below the line, switch the ground off to inspect it, or try another observing time.
What you should see

The ground covers the part of the celestial sphere below your local horizon. An object above the line has positive altitude.

Choose map layers and catalogs / Step 19

Set the faintest stars to draw

Start here Open Sky Atlas Settings and click Stars in the left column.

  1. Drag Limiting magnitude (1). Move right to include fainter stars, left to keep the brighter ones. Read the estimated star count and download size under Star catalog download before confirming.
  2. Click OK at the bottom of the dialog to apply. If a download starts, keep using the map while it runs. Zoom in to see the fainter stars as they become available.
What you should see

At a modest limit the estimate names Hipparcos. Deeper limits may require larger Tycho-2 or Gaia data downloads. Cancel in the settings dialog restores your saved values.

Set which deep-sky objects appear →Zoom in to inspect the field → Browse all steps

Choose map layers and catalogs / Step 20

Choose which deep-sky objects and names appear

Start here Open Sky Atlas Settings.

  1. Click Deep-Sky Objects (1). Under Catalogs to show, check Messier (2) for the Orion Nebula example. Select any additional catalogs you need.
  2. Drag Label limiting magnitude (3) to the right to label fainter objects. The separate Object limiting magnitude controls which markers appear.
  3. Click OK (4) to apply. Enabling a catalog for the first time may start a download.
What you should see

The map displays the enabled objects within the chosen brightness limits. Zoom in to bring out labels in crowded fields.

Look up a catalog object →Set the star limit → Browse all steps

Choose map layers and catalogs / Step 22

Change the Milky Way background

Start here Open Sky View options.

  1. Check Milky Way (1) to show the background.
  2. Open Preset (2) and try Dark sky or Atlas. Drag Opacity (3) left to make the backdrop less prominent, or right to bring it forward.
  3. Click in the sky to close the popup.
What you should see

The haze and dark lanes behind the stars change in strength. Object positions stay fixed while you adjust the background.

Place your own image on the sky / Step 27

Name the survey and add an image

Start here Create a new survey, or click a survey that you already have. Keep a registered image handy. Registration means the file contains the sky position for its pixels.

  1. Click Name (1) and give the collection a recognizable name. The example uses Lagoon field. Optionally choose a Filter (2), such as Ha for a hydrogen-alpha image.
  2. Click Add Image… (3).
What you should see

A file picker opens. CAst accepts FITS, XISF, PNG and TIFF overlays.

Select the image file → Browse all steps

Place your own image on the sky / Step 28

Select the registered image

Start here The file picker opened from Add Image. Use a plate-solved file. For a PNG or TIFF, a matching FITS sidecar can supply the sky coordinates.

  1. Browse to the image or enter its name (1). The illustrated example selects Lagoon.png and keeps its matching Lagoon.fits coordinate file in the same folder.
  2. Click Open (2) and wait for the import. If the image lacks a usable coordinate solution, the app attempts a plate solve. A solved FITS or XISF image is the simplest starting point.
What you should see

The file name appears in the survey’s Images list after import succeeds.

Save the completed survey →Choose another image → Browse all steps

Place your own image on the sky / Step 29

Check the imported image and save

Start here Import an image and wait for its name to appear.

  1. Check the Images list (1) under the correct survey name. To add another exposure or tile, repeat Add Image….
  2. Click OK (2) to save the collection and return to the atlas.
What you should see

Your named collection is available in the Survey list. Its images are drawn at the coordinates supplied by each image’s sky solution.

Display and blend the survey → Browse all steps

Place your own image on the sky / Step 30

Show an existing survey and set its opacity

Start here Open Custom Sky Map. At least one survey must contain an imported image.

  1. Open Survey (1) and choose your collection. The example uses Lagoon field (Ha). Check Show custom sky overlays (2) to display it.
  2. Drag Opacity (3) left to blend the image into the atlas, or right to make it stronger. Click away from the popup when you have finished.
  3. Search for the target in your image and zoom in. Choosing a survey keeps your current view, so you may need to travel to its field.
What you should see

The image appears in its sky footprint. If you cannot see a difference, confirm the right survey is selected, its checkbox is on, and the map is centered on its target.

Compare the registered image with the atlas →Add another field → Browse all steps

Place your own image on the sky / Step 31

Compare the overlay with the atlas alone

Start here Display your survey and zoom toward its target. The example uses the Lagoon Nebula, M8.

  1. Find the marked field (1). Use Atlas only and With image above the tutorial screenshot to inspect the change at fixed scale.
  2. To repeat it in CAst, toggle Show custom sky overlays in Custom Sky Map while keeping the view on the same sky position.
What you should see

The Lagoon Nebula and nearby catalog markers stay in the same positions. The registered image adds fine structure and many faint stars inside the footprint.
